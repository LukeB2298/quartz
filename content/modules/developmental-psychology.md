# Developmental Psychology

Developmental psychology studies how and why humans change throughout their lives, examining physical, cognitive, social, and emotional development.

## Major theories

### Piaget's stages of cognitive development
| Stage | Age Range | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| Sensorimotor | 0–2 years | Object permanence, sensory exploration |
| Preoperational | 2–7 years | Symbolic thinking, egocentrism |
| Concrete operational | 7–11 years | Conservation, logical reasoning |
| Formal operational | 11+ years | Abstract reasoning, hypothetical thinking |

### Attachment theory
- **[[Bowlby's Attachment Theory]]** — innate need for proximity to caregiver
- **[[Ainsworth's Strange Situation]]** — secure, anxious-avoidant, anxious-resistant attachment styles
- **Internal working models** — early relationships shape later social expectations

### Vygotsky's sociocultural theory
- **Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)** — gap between what a child can do alone vs. with help
- **Scaffolding** — structured support that fades as competence grows
- **Cultural tools** — language and symbols mediate cognitive development

### Erikson's psychosocial stages
Eight stages from trust vs. mistrust (infancy) to integrity vs. despair (old age), each with a crisis to resolve.

## Lifespan perspective

Development does not stop at adulthood. Key adult transitions include:
- **Identity formation** — [[Marcia's Identity Statuses]]
- **Intimacy vs. isolation** — Erikson's young adult stage
- **Cognitive aging** — fluid vs. crystallized intelligence
- **Successful aging** — activity theory vs. disengagement theory

## Research methods in developmental psychology

- **Cross-sectional designs** — compare age groups at one time point
- **Longitudinal designs** — follow same individuals over time
- **Sequential designs** — combine cross-sectional and longitudinal approaches
