# Milgram's Obedience Studies

Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments (1961–1963) are among the most famous and controversial studies in psychology. They investigated how far people would go in obeying instructions from an authority figure, even when those instructions involved harming another person.

## The procedure

1. Participants were told they were in a "learning and memory" study
2. They were assigned the role of "teacher" (the "learner" was a confederate)
3. The teacher was instructed to administer electric shocks for each wrong answer
4. Shocks increased in 15-volt increments, from 15V to 450V
5. The experimenter (authority figure) prompted continued participation with standardized prods
6. **No real shocks were administered** — the learner's responses were pre-recorded

## Key findings

- **65%** of participants administered the maximum 450V shock
- Most participants showed visible distress but continued when prompted
- Obedience was influenced by:
  - **Proximity of the victim** — closer = less obedience
  - **Proximity of authority** — closer = more obedience
  - **Institutional context** — Yale University added legitimacy
  - **Group effects** — seeing others disobey reduced obedience

## Ethical concerns

- **Deception** — participants were not told the true purpose
- **Psychological harm** — participants experienced extreme stress
- **Right to withdraw** — prods made this difficult in practice

These studies contributed to the development of modern ethical guidelines in research (see [[Research Methods]]).

## Interpretations

- **Agentic state** — Milgram argued people shift responsibility to authority
- **Engaged followership** — Haslam & Reicher's modern reinterpretation: participants identify with the authority's scientific project
- **Situationism** — the power of situational factors over disposition
