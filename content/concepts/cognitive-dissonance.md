# Cognitive Dissonance

Cognitive dissonance is the mental discomfort experienced when holding two or more conflicting beliefs, values, or attitudes, or when behavior contradicts beliefs. The theory was proposed by Leon Festinger in 1957.

## The theory

Festinger proposed that dissonance is psychologically uncomfortable, creating a drive to reduce it. People can reduce dissonance by:

1. **Changing their behavior** to align with their beliefs
2. **Changing their beliefs** to align with their behavior
3. **Adding new cognitions** that justify the discrepancy
4. **Minimizing the importance** of the conflict

## Classic study: Festinger & Carlsmith (1959)

Participants completed boring tasks, then were paid either $1 or $20 to tell the next participant the task was "fun."

- **$1 group** reported liking the task more
- **$20 group** reported honestly that it was boring

The $1 group experienced dissonance between "the task was boring" and "I lied for only $1," so they changed their attitude to reduce dissonance. The $20 group had sufficient justification ($20) and felt no need to change their attitude.

## Applications

- **Post-decision dissonance** — after making a choice, people devalue the rejected option
- **Effort justification** — people value outcomes more when they invested more effort
- **Induced compliance** — when forced to act against beliefs, attitude change occurs

## Modern extensions

- **Self-affirmation theory** — Steele's alternative: people reduce dissonance by affirming self-integrity rather than changing attitudes
- **Hypocrisy paradigm** — making people aware of their own hypocrisy motivates behavior change
